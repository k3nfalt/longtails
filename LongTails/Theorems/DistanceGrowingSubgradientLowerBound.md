# A two-dimensional lower bound with interpolation and zero initial variance

Does a small stochastic subgradient at initialization control the cost of convex optimization when second moments can grow with distance? Even a deterministic initial gradient and a common minimizer of all the smooth sample losses leave the full growth penalty necessary in dimension two.

**Theorem.** Let $R$ bound the initial distance to a minimizer and let the full second moment of an unbiased stochastic subgradient at $x$ be at most $G^2+B^2\|x-x_0\|^2$. Let $d\ge2$, $G,R,\varepsilon>0$, $B\ge0$, $65536\varepsilon\le GR$, and $0<\delta\le1/8$. Any adaptive randomized algorithm using at most
$$
\left\lfloor
\frac{R^2(G^2+B^2R^2)\log(1/\delta)}{2^{28}\varepsilon^2}
\right\rfloor
$$
oracle calls can fail the requirement of returning a measurable feasible point with objective gap at most $\varepsilon$ with probability at least $1-\delta$. In particular, for measurable outputs on the hard pair at least one instance has probability **greater** than $\delta$ of gap **greater** than $\varepsilon$. The quantifiers include zero calls, the displayed endpoint, and every smaller budget. The hard instance can depend on the algorithm and its budget and is independent of the realized seed. The regime includes, for example, $d=2$, $R=G=1$, $\varepsilon=2^{-16}$, $\delta=1/8$ and every $B\ge0$. [Lean theorem](DistanceGrowingSubgradientLowerBound.lean) · [Setup](../Setups/InterpolatedDistanceGrowingSubgradient.lean) · [Hard instances](../Proofs/BlumGladyshevHardInstance.lean)

The pair characterizes the high-confidence oracle complexity up to universal constants throughout this regime. The upper bound covers all convex objectives and jointly measurable stochastic subgradient oracles satisfying the moment bound on a closed convex domain. The lower-bound setup further requires an unconstrained domain, convex infinitely differentiable sample losses, their population objective, a common sample minimizer within radius $R$, and a common sample gradient at $x_0$. It imposes no algorithm restriction. The upper algorithm receives exactly the same query interface. In the lower construction the projection oracle is the identity. Upper and lower budgets each charge one call for either a projection or a sampled first-order reply. The growth term dominates when $BR\gg G$; the constant-moment statistical term survives at $B=0$. Both bounds use the same tunable failure probability. [Upper theorem and comparisons](DistanceGrowingSubgradientUpperBound.md) · [Algorithm](../Algorithms/LocalizedClippedSubgradient.lean)

## Construction and proof

Choose two orthonormal directions and write $t,s$ for their coordinates relative to $x_0$, divided by $R$. With $V(z)=\sqrt{1+z^2}-1$, take the two population objectives
$$
F_\pm(x)=512\varepsilon\,[V(t-1/2)+V(t\pm s)].
$$
Every sample contains the first term. An independent Bernoulli event of probability $q=2^{24}\varepsilon^2/[R^2(G^2+B^2R^2)]$ includes the second term scaled by $1/q$. In each instance all sample losses attain zero at $(t,s)=(1/2,\mp1/2)$. They give exactly the same nonzero gradient at the origin because $V'(0)=0$. The second term models a randomly observed homogeneous fitting penalty. The first term fits an always-observed target. Both fits can be satisfied simultaneously. Replacing the second coordinate direction by any orthogonal direction embeds the construction in all higher dimensions. The population objectives are nonlinear and the second term couples the coordinates.

The spatial control comes from $|V'(z)|\le\min(1,|z|)$. With $a=512\varepsilon/R$, the common gradient has norm at most $a$ and the **unscaled** additional field has norm at most both $2a$ and $4a\|x-x_0\|/R$. The uniform bound controls its contribution against $G^2$ and the distance bound controls its contribution against $B^2\|x-x_0\|^2$. Combining both inequalities gives the required full second moment for **every** query, including points arbitrarily far away. Simply replacing a uniform variance parameter by its value at radius $R$ in a testing construction would violate the smaller bound at the anchor. [Moment proof](../Proofs/BlumGladyshevMoments.lean) · [Sample law and admissibility](../Proofs/BlumGladyshevOracle.lean)

If $F_\pm(x)\le\varepsilon$, each of its two $V$ terms is at most $1/512$, forcing its scalar argument to have absolute value at most $1/8$. A common successful output would have $t\ge3/8$ and both $|t+s|,|t-s|\le1/8$, which is impossible. On an all-common prefix the transcripts of the two actual executions agree for every internal seed. The proof uses a prefix of length $n+1$ for a budget $n$, an inessential extra sample that also handles $n=0$. Its probability $(1-q)^{n+1}$ exceeds $2\delta$ at the stated budget. A union bound gives the contradiction without restricting query locations, the magnitude of inputs, or the output rule. ## Prior results and limits

Alacaoglu, Malitsky and Wright's initial-point B–G condition and their weighted-average bound motivate the convex problem. The full-moment model can arise from smooth sample losses with square-integrable random smoothness constants and a nonsmooth Lipschitz term. The extra interpolation and anchor conditions strengthen the converse. They are dispensable for the upper bound. Fazla et al. use distance-dependent hiding for nonconvex gradient-norm stationarity. Their nonconvex chain and initial-gap parameter do not give a convex objective-gap lower bound. [Alacaoglu et al., Assumption 3.1 and Corollary 3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09951v3#S3) · [Fazla et al., §3 and Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16620v1)

Novoselov et al. analyze oracle models under interpolation. In their whole-objective masking construction, the sample gradient at a nonoptimal anchor takes two different values. Our construction masks only a transverse field. The common term moves the optimizer away from the anchor, while the unobserved field chooses the relevant point in its minimum set. Known fast rates under a prescribed uniform sample smoothness constant $L_s$ remain applicable at their own parameter scale. Here $V''(z)=(1+z^2)^{-3/2}$, and the rare loss has gradient Lipschitz constant of order $\varepsilon/(qR^2)$, or $(G^2+B^2R^2)/\varepsilon$. At constant confidence the benchmark $L_sR^2/\varepsilon$ permits this lower bound. The cited expected guarantee does not establish the same dependence on $\delta$. A claim of hardness with an additional accuracy-independent unit cap on sample smoothness would be stronger than the result proved here. [Novoselov et al., §4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38354) · [Attia et al., NeurIPS 2025, §1 and Table 1](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/97335d45cab57ecb52727c4380ca14a4-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**Why two dimensions matter.** On a line, query the deterministic anchor derivative once. If it vanishes, the anchor minimizes every sample loss. Otherwise its sign orients the coordinate toward a common minimizer. Every convex sample derivative between the anchor and that minimizer then lies in $[-G,0]$. Consider a midpoint test on a bracket of width $W$. An observed negative derivative certifies that the point is to the left of the minimizer. Otherwise one can retain the left half. If this latter choice raises the minimum objective over the bracket by more than $e$, convexity implies a population derivative of magnitude greater than $e/W$ pointing right. A negative sample then occurs with probability greater than $e/(GW)$. Repeating the test $\lceil(GW/e)\log(1/\delta_j)\rceil$ times controls this loss with failure probability $\delta_j$. Starting with width $R$, choose $e_j$ proportional to $\varepsilon\,2^{-j/2}$ with sum at most $\varepsilon/2$, and $\delta_j=\delta\,2^{-j-1}$. The left endpoint never passes the true minimizer. At width $\varepsilon/(2G)$ it is within $\varepsilon/2$ in objective of the best remaining point on its left-to-minimizer segment. Summing the tests gives $O((GR/\varepsilon)\log(1/\delta))$ calls on this smaller univariate class. This scalar calculation explains the transverse constraint. The formal lower theorem concerns $d\ge2$. The broad upper theorem makes no samplewise interpolation or anchor assumption.

The result concerns stochastic first-order information, exact projections, a known radius, and a known distance-growth envelope. At $GR\le\varepsilon$ the anchor already suffices; the lower bound leaves the intervening coarse-accuracy range open. The hard pair has two sample types; the setup and the upper theorem allow general sample spaces.
