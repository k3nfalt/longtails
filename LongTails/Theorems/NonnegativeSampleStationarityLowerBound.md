# An oracle lower bound for nonnegative smooth sample losses

For an expected risk $F=\mathbb E\ell_\xi$ with nonnegative, individually $L$-smooth sample losses and $F(0)\leq\Delta$, how many fresh examples are necessary to return a point with expected population-gradient norm at most $\varepsilon$? A call chooses two points and receives both sample gradients on the same fresh example. This gives the information needed for variance reduction. Every randomized algorithm using this interface is eligible. [General setup](../Setups/NonnegativeSmoothSampleLoss.lean) · [Euclidean specialization](../Setups/NonnegativeSmoothSampleLossEuclidean.lean)

Write $R=L\Delta/\varepsilon^2$ and $n=\lfloor\sqrt R/2^{32}\rfloor$. For $L,\Delta,\varepsilon>0$ and $\sqrt R\geq2^{32}$, every algorithm fails on some admissible risk and oracle at every budget from zero through $n^3$, inclusive. The hard instance is fixed independently of its realized random seed. Success requires measurability, integrability and $\mathbb E\|\nabla F(X)\|\leq\varepsilon$ for the point actually returned. The companion theorem gives $O((1+R)^{3/2})$ paired calls to this accuracy, so the large-$R$ worst-case complexity is $\Theta(R^{3/2})$. Both bounds count fresh paired calls, equivalently twice as many individual gradient evaluations when both are charged. [Lower theorem](NonnegativeSampleStationarityLowerBound.lean) · [Upper theorem and algorithm](NonnegativeSampleStationarity.md)

The lower setup specializes the upper specification to a Euclidean space of dimension $n m D$ and sample space of size $m=[512(n+1)]^2$. The public width is $D=2^{21}(2n^3+1)n m^2+1+e$ for any nonnegative integer $e$. With $e=0$ the sufficient dimension is $O(n^{11})$. The large numerical threshold comes from conservative smoothness and scaling estimates. Taking $\Delta=\varepsilon=1,L=2^{64}$ gives $n=1$, and increasing $L$ gives unbounded stage counts. Fixed dimension and fixed sample counts remain open here.

## Hard sample losses

Split the domain into $n$ blocks with $m$ disjoint rows of $D$ coordinates per block. Fix an unknown coordinate in each row. The unit direction of each stage sums its row vectors with coefficient $1/\sqrt m$. The mean objective uses the squared norm and gated links, with each link controlled by the alignment in the preceding block. Alignment uses $x/\sqrt{1+\|x\|^2}$, which bounds what a query of arbitrarily large norm can learn. A block with small alignment following fully opened gates witnesses a population gradient norm exceeding a constant before rescaling. [Frame](../Proofs/SampleLossFrame.lean) · [Gradient barrier](../Proofs/SampleLossGradient.lean)

The main issue is enforcing nonnegativity without exhausting the expected initial loss. A row gradient must reveal only one coordinate of the next hidden direction. For a uniformly sampled row $i$, form the centered difference between the stage direction and its row vector multiplied by $\sqrt m$. Its expectation vanishes. Introduce these differences recursively into a single scalar perturbation, updating $s$ to $(1-a)s+a b$ at each incoming gate $a$, where $b$ is a normalized row difference. At an unopened suffix the hidden part of the mean link cancels exactly in the sample potential on a neighborhood of the query. Hence the full gradient at either query point is determined by the exposed prefix and one row. Across arbitrarily many simultaneously active gates the perturbation has magnitude at most $1+\sqrt m$. A shift of order $n+\sqrt m$ makes every component nonnegative. [Sample loss](../Proofs/SampleLossChain.lean) · [Neighborhood identity](../Proofs/SampleLossFrontier.lean)

For comparison, insert the raw row into every ordinary chain link and add the resulting links. In block $j$ choose the projected vector $(u_j+e_{ji})/2$, where $u_j$ is the unit stage direction and $e_{ji}$ is row $i$’s hidden basis vector. Their inner product is $1/\sqrt m$. Thus the projected norm squared is $(1+1/\sqrt m)/2$, well below one, and the inverse radial image has bounded norm. Every gate opens, every row alignment is at least $\sqrt m/2$, and the quadratic term costs only $O(n)$. For each row this forces an order-$n\sqrt m$ shift to make the additive sample loss nonnegative. Allowing a different shift per row still incurs this order in the average initial risk. The recursive perturbation removes this factor while preserving the mean links.

Bounding the perturbation’s magnitude alone would leave open whether its gradient Lipschitz constant grows with $n$. The gate derivative is bounded by a constant times the square root of the gate. Its square can therefore be absorbed by the factor $1-a$ damping the previous perturbation. Iterating the second-order remainder estimate costs only the sum of the squared changes in the individual blocks. This proves $O(1+\sqrt m)$ raw individual smoothness, including away from typical algorithm trajectories. [Mixture estimate](../Proofs/SampleLossMixture.lean) · [Global smoothness](../Proofs/SampleLossRegularity.lean)

For a fixed algorithm seed and sample tape, expose all preceding stages and every current row index occurring in an epoch of $n^2$ calls. These rows contribute at most $n/\sqrt m<1/512$ to any normalized query. Counting the unseen hidden positions controls both points in every pair and the unqueried output. With at least half the hidden tables still obstructing stationarity, summing the true residuals over tables and then integrating over both seeds selects one seed-independent instance. The instance may depend on the algorithm and its budget. Scaling the constructed loss controls its initial value, gradient Lipschitz constant and gradient threshold simultaneously. ## Closest lower bounds

Arjevani et al., Theorem 3, equation (24), and Proposition 1 in §5, establish $\Omega(\Delta \bar L\sigma/\varepsilon^3+\sigma^2/\varepsilon^2)$ hardness for bounded variance and mean-squared smoothness. A bound on the mean-squared gradient difference allows individual samples to be much less smooth. Their theorem also measures the population optimality gap. Substituting a noise scale of order $\sqrt{L\Delta}$ does not certify nonnegative sample potentials, common individual smoothness, and the initial risk bound together. [Arjevani et al., corrected v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02365v2)

Peng, Tang and Jia, Theorem 3.1, obtain $\Omega(m+\sqrt m L\Delta/\varepsilon^2)$ for $m$ components under individual smoothness, with the expected-gradient-norm extension in Appendix F. At $m\asymp R$ this has the same exponent as the bound here. Wu et al., Theorem 3.2, treat higher orders $p\geq2$ and give a related radial chain in Appendix C, Lemma C.1. Proposition C.1(ii) of Peng et al. explicitly controls the average-objective gap. Their incremental oracles allow component selection and their scale is the population gap $F(0)-\inf F$. With nonnegative samples the average difference from component infima may be arbitrarily larger than that gap. For example, $\ell_\pm(x)=L(x\mp B)^2/2$ gives zero population gap at the origin, while the initial risk is $LB^2/2$, even after each component is shifted to have infimum zero. Consequently a population-gap theorem alone cannot be substituted into this comparison. The bounded recursive perturbation retains row hiding, global individual smoothness, and a sufficiently small component shift simultaneously. This establishes the cubic lower rate for the specified sample-risk class. Component-selection or function-value lower bounds for the new construction and optimal ambient dimension remain open here. [Peng et al., preprint v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.00045v3) · [Wu et al., preprint v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28202v2)

The shift cost can also be seen directly in Wu et al., equation (36). At a stage with unit mean and row sign vectors $u,s$ such that $\langle u,s\rangle\geq-1/2$, choose $x=2(u+s)$. Both radial alignments are at least $1/\sqrt5$, above the gate-opening threshold $1/4$, and $\|x\|^2\leq16$. A fixed row has independent column signs relative to the hidden direction, with positive mean $\rho$. A union bound over rows and stages gives tables satisfying this alignment condition everywhere when the width is sufficiently large. Each row can then attain raw value at most $16n-n/(\sqrt5\rho)$, at a point depending on that row. At small $\rho$, shifting the original components individually to nonnegativity can therefore cost order $n/\rho$ in initial risk. [Wu et al., Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28202v2#A3)
