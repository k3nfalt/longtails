# Why a learned two-tap filter changes the time to separate images

Learning two numbers before a linear classifier changes gradient descent even though it preserves the set of possible classifiers. We prove finite separation for the original, smooth logistic loss on arbitrarily many signed examples, starting before interpolation. We also derive an exact two-step filter equation and use it to investigate the large-step behavior in Figure 1 of Tyurin. On a specified reproduction with 5,000 airplane and automobile images, the first all-correct iterate arrives at 23,676 with the learned filter and 319,077 with plain GD. The opposite ordering occurs on MNIST 7 versus 8. All counts below concern strict training margins. [Tyurin, ICML 2026, §1.1 and Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11587v2#S1.SS1)

## The theorem

Write the signed examples as $x_i=y_i b_i\in\mathbb R^d$, and let $S$ be a one-position cyclic shift. The trainable filter is $(a,b)$, the readout is $v$, and the margin is $m_i=\langle a x_i+b Sx_i,v\rangle$. The loss $F$ is the mean of $\log(1+e^{-m_i})$. One oracle call returns its exact full negative gradient at the queried parameters. Both layers take one simultaneous step of the same positive constant size. The method returns its last iterate. The setup enforces this update and output rule while leaving the step size free. [Setup](../Setups/TwoTapLogisticSeparation.lean) · [Algorithm](../Algorithms/TwoTapLogisticGd.lean) · [Theorem and explicit budget](TwoTapLogisticSeparation.lean)

Assume $\|x_i\|\le R$. The two feature collections $(I+S)x_i$ and $(I-S)x_i$ each admit a unit-ball separator of signed margin at least $\gamma>0$. Their separators can differ. These are two static linear feasibility tests. They protect the readout gradient even when one filter channel vanishes. A raw separator that uses only the constant image direction can lose all of its margin after differencing, so the assumption really strengthens raw separability. It is sufficient for the general guarantee. As a dimension-independent family, take a real nonconstant Fourier plane on which $S$ rotates by $\omega\in(0,\pi)$, a unit vector $u$ in it, and arbitrary bounded signed rows with $\langle u,x_i\rangle\ge m>0$. Applying the inverse transposes of $I\pm S$ on that plane and normalizing gives view margins $2m|\cos(\omega/2)|$ and $2m|\sin(\omega/2)|$. The rows can have arbitrary components orthogonal to $u$ and can couple all coordinates.

For initial tap energy $U=a_0^2+b_0^2$ and readout energy $V=\|v_0\|^2$, require either $F_0\le\log 2-\delta$, or $F_0\le1$ and $U-V\ge\delta>0$. The first condition allows many misclassified rows. The second includes the identity filter with a zero readout. An initialization condition matters since both zero layers give a stationary point. The numeric cutoff 1 is a convenient sufficient bound. The algorithm receives $n,d,R,\gamma,\delta$ and the initialization. It has no access to the training rows, separating directions, principal components or stopping flags.

With the quantities in the linked budget, $1/L$ is the constant step and
$$
 T=\left\lceil \bigl(1+80n(2+R)KL/\gamma^2\bigr)\log\bigl(1+4(2+R)(1+Z)\bigr)\right\rceil
$$
full-gradient calls suffice for a strictly positive margin on **every** training row. The proof also gives this at every later iterate. At fixed data geometry and initialization this is $O(n[1+\log\log(8n)])$ calls, each costing one full data pass. The explicit bound retains the dependence on the margins, starting energies and loss or surplus allowance.

The proof must control a bilinear predictor with unbounded parameters. Put $Q=n^{-1}\sum_i(1+e^{m_i})^{-1}$ and $z=a^2+b^2+\|v\|^2$. Orthogonality of the sum and difference of a vector and its shift gives $\|\nabla F\|^2\ge(\gamma/2)^2(a^2+b^2)Q^2$. The imbalance $\|v\|^2-a^2-b^2$ changes only at second order and its cumulative variation is paid for by loss descent. The initial conditions then keep the tap energy away from zero. A scalar logistic tangent inequality applied to $z+2\eta F$ bounds the **square** of this augmented energy by an initial term plus a multiple of $\log^3(2/F)$. Thus $zF$ stays bounded independently of the target accuracy. This closes the descent argument at a single fixed step before any separator has been reached. Until $F\le1/(4n)$, the energy estimate yields an additive increase of $1/F$. Every individual loss is at most $nF$, and the pointwise bound $\ell\le(1+\ell)q$ also gives $F\le(1+nF)Q$. Combining the two inequalities yields geometric growth of $1/F+n/[(2+R)(1+Z)]$. This second estimate reduces the earlier $n\log^{3/2}(8n)$ budget to the logarithm of that radius factor. At that threshold even one nonpositive margin would contribute too much loss. ## What happens at the step sizes of Figure 1

In Section 1, the paper reports approximately 330,000 and 20,000 iterations using the best rates in the respective grids. Our replication fixes a new subset and initialization. It samples 2,500 rows without replacement from each of training classes 0 and 1 using NumPy's default generator, seed 20261125, in class order. It flattens RGB images channel first, centers pixels by $2p/255-1$, and gives class 0 negative sign. PyTorch with the same seed draws the readout uniformly on $[-1/\sqrt d,1/\sqrt d]$ then the two taps on $[-1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2]$. Plain and learned runs share the readout. The 13-column FP32 reproduction uses an H100 with TF32 disabled, counting the starting point as step zero. The grids are $10^{-3},\ldots,10^2$ and $2^{-6},\ldots,1$. A pass includes all 5,000 examples.

The CIFAR rows have normalized sum/difference view margins at least 0.00923 and 0.00693 respectively, giving $\gamma=0.009$ for the unnormalized views in Lean. Their largest raw norm is below 55, and $F_0=0.69140$. For $\delta=0.001$ the worst-case formula gives a step around $1.3\cdot10^{-14}$ and a budget around $1.3\cdot10^{38}$. It establishes convergence on these data but is far too conservative to predict the experimental step $1/8$ or its first hit.

**Why the filter turns.** Write $g_t=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i/(1+e^{m_{i,t}})$ and $h=(g_{t+1}-g_t)/2$. The readout update is $\eta(a_t I+b_t S)g_t$. For $\sigma\in\{-1,1\}$ its coupled tap coordinate $c_{\sigma,t}=a_t+\sigma b_t$ obeys the exact recurrence
$$
c_{\sigma,t+2}
=\left(1-\tfrac{\eta^2}{2}\|(I+\sigma S)g_t\|^2\right)c_{\sigma,t}
 +\eta\left\langle(I+\sigma S)(g_t+g_{t+1}),v_{t+1}\right\rangle .
$$
Large oscillations along spatially smooth gradients damp the sum channel while retaining the difference channel. The forcing term on the right determines whether this damping wins. In the exact three-term expansion of the signed area $\det(c_t,c_{t+2}-c_t)$, the alternating contribution is $-\eta^2(a_t^2-b_t^2)\langle h,Sh\rangle$. In the CIFAR reproduction over steps 256–512, the alternating gradient's normalized lag-one correlation is 0.9983. For each pair with nonzero determinant, multiplying each area term by $\operatorname{atan2}(\det(c_t,c_{t+2}),c_t^\top c_{t+2})/\det(c_t,c_{t+2})$ allocates the actual angle change across the three forces. The three accumulated angle contributions are $+1.181$, $-0.011$, and $-1.403$ radians, yielding the observed negative turn. In a paired-update intervention which preserves the endpoint filter norm and all other angular forces, retaining half of this last term delays the first hit to 61,286. Removing it gives no hit by 100,000. The intervention's unit-multiplier control gives 23,650 under its own arithmetic. [Exact channel and signed-area identities](../Proofs/LogisticSpatialResponse.lean) · [Identity for the actual logistic update](../Proofs/TwoTapLogisticDescent.lean)

The changing channel also weights the gradients of the hard examples. CIFAR row 2800 has the largest hard-margin dual coefficient in the raw fit and remains the worst example late in training. From 23,000 to 23,431 its margin rises from about $-0.087$ to just above zero. The sum-channel and difference-channel readout contributions are $+0.029$ and $+0.123$, while changing the tap direction contributes about $-0.065$. Deleting that row advances separation substantially more than deleting a random row. Radial growth and the final hard-example geometry matter alongside rotation. A two-step extrapolation started after 128 genuine updates predicts the odd-iterate crossing near 22,685. Validated high-precision continuation from initialization gives the **exact-real** first hit at 23,431 for $\eta=1/8$. All six exact-real plain runs are excluded through at least 24,690, a factor exceeding 1.053. Exact-real and FP32 trajectories differ at these step sizes.

**Why increasing the plain step fails to close the gap.** With $z=w/\eta$ the raw update is $z^+=z+X^\top q/n$ and $q_i=(1+e^{\eta x_i^\top z})^{-1}$. Once a posterior saturates, increasing $\eta$ barely changes that row's normalized update. The leading image directions oscillate rapidly, while motion orthogonal to those directions is needed for separation. A clock computed from the training rows and initialization follows 64 fast steps in the leading 32 uncentered covariance directions with the complementary coordinates frozen. It advances the complement by one implicit convex step for the mean logistic loss over those phases, spanning up to 1,024 physical updates. For raw steps 1, 10 and 100 it predicts crossings at 340,512, 317,408 and 321,056. The three measured values are in the table. With only four fast directions the step-10 forecast is 321,056. These rounded macrostep forecasts use no future GD state.

There is a quantitative check on why the forecast excludes a separator. A nonnegative combination $b=\sum_i\lambda_i x_i$ chosen to cancel the fast directions must have positive projection on any strictly separating weight. For instance, at step 128,000 with $\eta=10$, the forecast complementary coordinates are 31.16 normalized units away from the cone that *could* separate for some choice of fast coordinates. The actual error projected on the corresponding unit witness is 4.20. Each step can increase this projection by at most $P=n^{-1}\sum_i[\langle b,x_i\rangle]_+$, or decrease it by at most the analogous $N$ using $-b$, before roundoff. For a block of $h$ steps with endpoint projections $a_0,a_h$, a score-rounding allowance $B$, and update errors added to $P,N$, the inequality
$$
N(a_0+B)+P(a_h+B)+hPN\le0
$$
excludes every interior iterate. It follows by combining the forward and backward linear bounds. The Lean lemma also allows individual sign-rounding allowances. [Window proof](../Proofs/LogisticRawClock.lean)

For the FP32 replication, data-only forecasts supplied the witnesses and endpoint tolerances before checking a direction at each 64-step boundary, after a 256-step initialization audit. The directional tolerance of one quarter of the forecast gap was specified before the audit. Accounting for the decoded pixels, the rounded gradient step and the reported FP32 classifier certifies exclusions through **262,144, 262,144, 262,144, 207,040, 212,096 and 209,344** respectively. Thus the verified conditional factor across all six rates is **greater than 8.74**, against the observed 13.48. A looser one-half directional tolerance could not certify four early intervals. An initial Euclidean projection witness that ignored score rounding could certify the mathematical signs of the stored FP32 weights, but failed on the reported scores. The revised witness selection includes a rounding allowance. The capacity argument allows arbitrary posterior weights in $[0,1]$. The radial bound used for the score-rounding allowance additionally uses the logistic inequality $s\,\mathrm{sigmoid}(-s)\le1$, checked on every finite FP32 input on this device. This check uses forward GD to obtain the sparse audit states. It confers no oracle savings on GD. Neither the 8.74 bound nor the full 13.48 observed ratio is an unconditional exact-real class-wide acceleration.

## When the ordering reverses

On all 12,116 MNIST training images labeled 7 or 8 the raw and normalized shifted-view margins are positive. The same constant-step theorem applies via the positive initial tap surplus (about 0.0572), even though $F_0=0.71155>\log 2$. The large-rate behavior differs. Unsigned images have a common brightness direction. When one class saturates, its signed class sum drives the update, and the two-step gain in the spatially constant channel can exceed one in modulus. Large parameters can then alternate between class-dominated directions while leaving some examples incorrect. A nonnegative data witness forces every separator to place more than 0.7924 of its norm outside the span of the two class means and their one-step forward/backward shifts. The four high-rate trajectories stay below 0.748 in that fraction through 38,500 steps. A six-dimensional alternating-class model constructed from the data predicts longest class-only runs of 17, 23, 24 and 25 pairs; the measured maxima between steps 16 and 6,799 are 16, 22, 24 and 24. Fast growth and accurate classification are distinct effects.

Across all seven learned rates the exact-real minimum first hit is **38,398**, at $1/16$. Plain GD at step 100 first succeeds at **6,578**, establishing at least a **5.837-fold** reversal. Setting every CIFAR image's mean brightness to $u$ preserves its entire difference view. The corresponding saturated brightness model predicts an unstable channel when $\eta u>2/\sqrt d$. For $u=1/8,1/4,1/2,1$ the predicted grid thresholds are $1/2,1/4,1/8,1/16$. The corresponding training runs crossed parameter magnitude $10^{12}$ at 39, 49, 54 and 62 updates, while the adjacent smaller rates stayed finite through 65,536. The native CIFAR experiment also has spatial modes beyond brightness.

Predictions recorded before running two additional grids ranked the learned filter first on CIFAR birds/dogs, and the plain model first on MNIST 0/4. The predicted best bird/dog learned run was 131,955 versus 124,376 observed, with all plain rates censored at 196,608. On MNIST 0/4 the corresponding plain/learned predictions were 3,359/13,219 versus 3,360/13,218 observed. The bird/dog forecast missed two slower successful rates. An earlier fixed-filter ridge forecast incorrectly ranked plain GD first on CIFAR frogs/ships. View margins alone do not predict acceleration, and two class moments alone do not determine the final hard row. The comparison is specific to the task, preprocessing, initialization and step grids. It makes no prediction of three-step learning, ten-class CIFAR accuracy or generalization to test images.

## Relation to existing results

Tyurin's Section 5 separation is for a two-example construction and a perceptron reduction. PR 253 analyzes random small initialization on two special rows, giving a high-probability $\widetilde O(\sqrt d/\mu)$ update bound under its radius condition. Its constant row satisfies $(I-S)x=0$ and falls outside the present two-view hypothesis. Its oracle makes two indexed calls per update. The present theorem gives a deterministic mean-gradient pass bound on a different class of interacting samples, including high-rank image data. It does not improve PR 253's $\mu$ rate. PR 258 obtains three-step separation on a constructed ranking family. Here the sigmoid remains finite, the samples can interact arbitrarily, and the two trainable filter parameters are shared across all coordinates. The uniform fixed-step energy estimate, forced channel equation and conditional image-specific comparison have different scopes. The new general result does not give a lower bound against unrestricted first-order methods. An algorithm that reads every row could solve a separating linear program directly; the setup supplies only a full gradient and asks specifically about GD. [Tyurin, §§2, 5 and 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11587v2) · [PR 253](https://github.com/k3nfalt/longtails/pull/253) · [PR 258](https://github.com/k3nfalt/longtails/pull/258)

Ji and Telgarsky's Assumption 3.5 and Theorem 3.6 use norm-controlled adaptive steps for unrestricted matrix layers. Restricting a full matrix to a two-tap convolution changes the gradient. Yun, Krishnan and Mobahi's Theorem 2 concerns gradient flow and orthogonally decomposable tensor networks. Cai et al.'s Assumption 1 for stable-phase convergence imposes regularity and near-one-homogeneity that the jointly scaled bilinear score violates. A theorem initialized below total logistic loss $\log 2$ already starts with every margin positive and gives no first-separator bound from the initializations here. [Ji and Telgarsky, ICLR 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02032) · [Yun et al., ICLR 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02501) · [Cai et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/835a0185f61867a1ea0f86155489839a-Abstract-Conference.html) · [Lyu and Li, ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05890)

A comparison under the very same oracle is possible if one is free to change the update. Hold the filter at $(1,0)$, start the readout at zero and query its gradient $g$. The sum-view witness gives a raw margin $m\ge\gamma/2$. Stop and retain the first iterate with $\|g\|<m/(2n)$, which certifies separation since one nonpositive row would imply $Q\ge1/(2n)$ and $\|g\|\ge mQ$. Before stopping, $Q\ge m/(2nR)$. The entropy inequality $\langle w,g\rangle\le Q\log(e/Q)$ and $\|g\|\le RQ$ bound squared-norm growth at step $\eta=\log(2enR/m)/R^2$, while a separating direction accumulates at least $\eta m\sum Q$. Combining the two estimates gives $\sum Q\le3R^2/m^2$, hence $O(n(R/m)^3)$ passes including the terminal check. Here $g$ denotes the **negative** gradient. The method-specific interest of the learned-update bound lies in controlling two moving layers and explaining the observed ordering. The unrestricted bound already has linear dependence on sample size at fixed geometry.

Large-step raw logistic results bound risk or the time of transition to permanent stable descent. A dataset's first strictly separating iterate can precede that transition. Recent two-iterate coarse-graining analyses already explain why oscillations can cause slow drift. The additional spatial calculation here identifies the signed lag-one correlation acting on an actual two-tap filter, tests the force by intervention, and checks how far an independent raw forecast stays from the separating cone.
