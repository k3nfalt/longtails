# Bounded-centre brackets for chi-square constrained DRO

The population objective is the supremum of the expected loss over nonnegative densities of mass one satisfying ½ E[(w−1)²] ≤ ρ. Each query returns its projection onto the closed convex domain, a loss in [0,B], and a subgradient of norm at most G there, for one fresh IID sample. A minimizer is within R of the initial point. The target is a measurable feasible output with expected excess objective at most ε. [Setup](../Setups/ChiSquareConstrainedDro.lean) · [Lean theorem](ChiSquareBoundedCentreDro.lean)

## Budget and comparison

When ε ≥ min(B,GR), the algorithm returns x₀ with zero calls. The objective takes values in [0,B] and is G-Lipschitz on the domain, so its gap at x₀ is bounded by both B and GR. Otherwise the budget, with c² = 1+2ρ and K = 1+⌊log₂(8B/ε)⌋, is

    K (⌈256 c² (G²R² + 35B²) / ε²⌉ + 5).

This is strictly smaller than the existing budget at **every valid parameter choice**. In the zero-call branch the old budget is at least 576. The old budget is (J+2)²(⌈65536c²(G²R²+B²(1+1/ρ))/ε²⌉+144), with J = ⌈log₂⌈8c(B+B/√ρ)/ε⌉⌉. In the positive branch its logarithm’s argument before rounding is at least 8B/ε, so K ≤ J+1. Coefficientwise, 256 ≤ 65536 and 256·35 = 8960 ≤ 65536(1+1/ρ). Monotonicity of the ceiling bounds the new per-bracket allowance by the old inner allowance including its additive 144. The latter is positive, and J+1 < (J+2)². These inequalities compare the padded integer budgets, including powers-of-two ties. [Existing bound](ChiSquareBracketedDro.lean)

Holding the other parameters fixed in the positive branch, the new budget stays bounded and positive as ρ ↓ 0. The old budget is Θ(ρ⁻¹ log²(1/ρ)), so the new-to-old ratio is Θ(ρ/log²(1/ρ)). As ρ → ∞ the new budget grows linearly with ρ. The old logarithm also grows with ρ, giving a ratio Θ(1/log²ρ). The ratio is identically zero in the zero-call branch.

## Method and proof

For a loss value z, centre a and scale λ > 0, maximize
wz+(1−w)a+λ(ρ−(w−1)²/2) over w ≥ 0. The maximizer is v = (z−a+λ)₊/λ. Write H for its value and Q = E H. This gives the perspective Φ(x,a−λ,cλ). Substitution of any feasible density gives Q ≥ L(x). The existing attained dual provides a threshold η and tail norm s at a minimizer. For s > 0 take λ = s/c and a = η+λ. At this point Q equals the dual score. Testing w = 1 gives ρλ ≤ B. Raising λ to at least ε/(8ρ) costs at most ε/8 since every affine function in the supremum has slope at most ρ. For s = 0 the loss is at most η almost surely and the same bias bound holds starting at centre η. Clamping the centre to [0,B] can only decrease H. The dyadic intervals [d,2d] starting at d = ε/(8ρ) cover this comparator.

Training uses T = ⌈256c²(G²R²+3B²)/ε²⌉ projected steps in each interval. Each step uses one projected point, loss and subgradient from the same reply. Starting at (x₀,0,d), use subgradients vg, 1−v and ρ−(v−1)²/2 with steps ε/(64c²G²), ε/(64c²) and ε/[64(ρ²+c²B²/d²)], respectively. Completing the square against a feasible density bounds E[(v−t)²] by 4(Q−L(x))/λ + 4ρ + 2(1−t)² for t = 0,1. Also |v−1| ≤ B/λ. With the steps in the algorithm, the sum of step-weighted second moments is at most 6ε/64 + (5/8)(Q−L⋆). The projected squared-distance inequality absorbs the latter term into its descent. The initial potential divided by T is at most ε/4. Summing and averaging gives a training error of at most (ε/4 + 2ε/8 + 6ε/64)/(2−5/8) = 19ε/44 on the comparator's interval.

The tournament scores an averaged candidate by η+c‖(ℓ−η)₊‖₂, where η is the average of a−λ across its training steps. This score lies between L and Q. The variable U = (ℓ−η)₊ lies in [a,b] with a = (−η)₊ and b = (B−η)₊. This gives b−a ≤ B and |U²−ab| ≤ (b−a)U. Subtracting ab before bounding the variance yields Var(U²) ≤ B² E U². For a batch of m independent losses, the empirical RMS thus has mean squared error at most B²/m, including the case E U² = 0. Multiplying by c gives mean squared error at most c²B²/m for the score.

In each match, the winner’s true score is at most either entrant’s true score plus the two absolute estimation errors. Round r uses mᵣ = ⌈1024c²B²(3/2)ʳ/ε²⌉ per survivor. Conditional on earlier rounds, each of the two estimates has expected absolute error at most (ε/32)(5/6)ʳ, since (3/2)(5/6)² ≥ 1. Summing twice this allowance gives at most 3ε/8. The proof conditions again on the training prefix, adds this cost to the good candidate’s ε/2 guarantee, and bounds the winner’s objective by its true score.

The first K slots hold the trained candidates in bracket order. The remaining slots hold copies of the first candidate. Each match advances its left entrant on an estimated tie. With P = 2^⌈log₂ K⌉ slots, the total useful cost is KT + Σ (P/2ʳ)mᵣ over r < log₂P. The real-valued geometric cost has ratio 3/4 and the rounding terms have ratio 1/2. Writing C = c²B²/ε², summing the two series bounds selection by P(4096C+2) ≤ K(8192C+4). Adding KT and using ⌈u⌉ ≤ u+1 gives the displayed allowance. The last match’s final reply fixes the winner. The terminal state retains its point through all remaining calls, which count against the budget. [Algorithm](../Algorithms/ChiSquareBoundedCentreDro.lean)

## Related work and limitations

The comparison covers the primary papers below, including those discussed in the existing contribution note. The repository has no lower-bound theorem for this setup. Its existing note attributes an Ω(ρ ε⁻²) sample lower bound in a normalized large-radius regime to Duchi and Namkoong. [Existing note](ChiSquareBracketedDro.md)

- Levy, Carmon, Duchi and Sidford use the same half-square convention in (3). Their Theorem 4 uses (16) to estimate gradients of penalized subproblems from 2ᴶn₀ IID samples per estimate, with a truncated geometric J. In that paper R bounds the diameter of the domain; here R bounds the initial distance to one robust minimizer and the domain may be unbounded. The upper bound displayed in their Theorem 4 on the total count of individual gradient evaluations, which holds with high probability, scales as (GR)²(ρB+ε log₂(B/ε))ε⁻³ times a squared logarithm in 1+ρB/ε². The theorem identifies a list containing an expected-accurate candidate. The following paragraph accounts for selection on additional samples. Table 1 and §8 record the gap to the sample lower bound. [Levy et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.05893)
- Duchi and Namkoong's Theorem 6 gives a statistical lower bound even when all n IID observations are available in full. For k=2 the radius convention is identical, p₂=1/(1+2ρ), and for fixed ρ>1/2 and sufficiently large n the first term in their maximum gives (M/32)√(2ρ/n). The cutoff terms in their minimum matter at small n. Shifting the linear losses on [−M,M] by M puts them in [0,2M] with G=1 and domain diameter 2M, so R=2M suffices. Each full observation simulates one reply to an adaptive query on this instance. Thus ε accuracy requires Ω(ρM²/ε²) samples in that regime. For ρ ≥ 1 and GR comparable to B, the upper bound has one extra logarithm in B/ε. The separate dependence on G, R and B remains open. [Duchi and Namkoong](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.08750)
- Namkoong and Duchi's Algorithm 1 samples one index from an adaptive distribution over n stored losses. Their radius in (1) is nρ in the present units. For k=2, Theorem 4 in §3.3 bounds the dual average regret by 2C₂√(C₂ nρ/(δT)) for normalized losses. At fixed floor and bounded C₂, this gives order nρ/ε² dual updates for error ε, alongside the primal regret. The theorem introduces the coordinate floor δ/n and an unnormalized weight set. This qualification matters when comparing an ε⁻² iteration rate with unrestricted densities. [Namkoong and Duchi](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2016/file/4588e674d3f0faf985047d4c3f13ed0d-Paper.pdf)
- Jin, Zhang, Wang and Wang use the half-square generator (Table 1) with a fixed divergence penalty λ in (3). Algorithm 1 uses a constant-size independent batch per stochastic gradient update. Corollary 3.6 gives O(G²(1+M²σ²/λ²)(L+MG²/λ)Δ ε⁻⁴) individual gradient evaluations for expected stationarity under smoothness assumptions. A penalty parameter alone does not specify the radius attained at the optimum, so that formula gives no constrained-ρ excess-risk rate. [Jin et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/164bf317ea19ccfd9e97853edc2389f4-Paper.pdf)
- Carmon and Hausler's (3) uses a unit divergence bound, so the generator for this problem is f(t)=(t−1)²/(2ρ). Their nonsmooth finite-sum method (Theorem 3 and Appendix D, Corollary 1) costs Õ(N(GR/ε)^(2/3)+(GR/ε)²) component evaluations. Its ball oracles combine full scans of N stored losses with individual evaluations. Corollary 1 uses logarithms of B_f B/ε. Assumption 5 states a global generator bound. On the finite simplex, the arguments Nqᵢ lie in [0,N], where the bound used in Proposition 3 can be taken as max{1, max(1,(N−1)²)/(2ρ)}. The quadratic generator itself is unbounded on the full half-line. This requires indexed access to the finite population. [Carmon and Hausler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.13225)
- Qi, Lyu, Chan, Bai and Yang treat a KL constraint with additional KL regularization. Algorithm 2 and its restarted RASCDRO variant in Algorithm 3 draw one index per inner step and use it at consecutive iterates. The proof of Lemma 5 initializes the estimator with a batch of order L_F²/(με₁). Corollary 2, on RASCDRO, gives O(ε⁻²) oracle complexity for the smooth convex objective. A KL radius specifies a different feasible set, so it has no general scalar conversion to the χ² radius here. [Qi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.05740)
- Im and Grigas' Theorem 5 and (15) give a high-probability feasibility gap with terms proportional to log(T)/√T plus an approximation tolerance. The dual term contains (1+√(2ρpaper/n))√(2ρpaper)/δ, with ρpaper=nρ. Their set relaxes mass normalization, imposes a coordinate floor δ/n and takes 0 < ρpaper < 1 in §3. Algorithm 6 uses one coordinate per dual update. Algorithms 3–4 use K sampled values per constraint to choose a constraint, then one sampled subgradient. K must satisfy Definition 2. [Im and Grigas](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16584)

The budget retains one logarithm in B/ε. All tuning parameters are public, the constants are loose, and the tournament spends fresh loss evaluations even on duplicated candidates. The accuracy criterion is in expectation.
