# Adaptive component queries in second-order convex finite sums

How much can a finite-sum optimizer gain by choosing which Hessian to inspect next? For convex minimization with mean-square Lipschitz component Hessians, the high-dimensional worst case requires
\[
\Omega\!\left(n+n^{6/7}(HR^3/\varepsilon)^{2/7}\right)
\]
component queries. The result applies to randomized algorithms that choose both the index and the point using the entire history, including all entries of previous Hessians. The two exponents were known for a restricted algorithm class. Extending the lower bound to unrestricted adaptive queries is the contribution. [Lean theorem](ConvexFiniteSumMeanSquareHessianLowerBound.lean) · [Setup](../Setups/ConvexFiniteSumMeanSquareHessian.lean)

## The oracle and the guarantee

Let \(F=n^{-1}\sum_i f_i\) on Euclidean \(d\)-space. Each component is twice continuously differentiable, the average is convex, and some minimizer has norm at most \(R\). For every pair of points the mean squared operator-norm change of the component Hessians is at most \(H^2\|x-y\|^2\). A call at \((i,x)\) returns the exact value, gradient, and full Hessian of \(f_i\). All five parameters \(n,d,H,R,\varepsilon\) are public. The algorithm has unrestricted internal computation and may query anywhere and return any point. Success requires a measurable output, an integrable objective gap, and expected gap at most \(\varepsilon\) under the algorithm and oracle laws.

Set \(s=(HR^3/(\sqrt n\,\varepsilon))^{2/7}\). For \(n\ge1,H\ge0,R>0,\varepsilon>0\) and
\(d\ge 2^{170}n^3(1+\lceil s\rceil)^{12}\), every algorithm at every fixed budget through \(\lfloor n(1+s)/2^{16}\rfloor\), inclusive, fails on some admissible deterministic oracle. The instance can depend on the policy and budget and is fixed before the policy draws its seed. Every positive radius and tolerance admits a finite dimension in this regime. The dimension bound is a sufficient hiding dimension. Its sharp dependence and the low-dimensional complexity remain open.

The model includes smooth generalized linear losses on nonorthogonal features, with a convex ridge penalty. For example, logistic losses on rows \(a_i\) have Hessian Lipschitz constants bounded by \(\sup|\ell'''|\,\|a_i\|^3\). A common positive quadratic makes the average attain a minimum. Component-specific indefinite quadratic terms summing to zero preserve both the average and the Hessian-increment bound. The lower bound itself uses nonlinear, interacting neighboring coordinates and holds even with individually convex hard components.

## How the hard instances work

A hidden ordered set of \(K\) coordinate axes supports an anchored path. A convex one-sided link is flat near zero, grows linearly at large positive arguments, and has three continuous derivatives. The links penalize successive coordinate differences, starting from the virtual coordinate \(4K\). With the last coordinate small, the sum of link costs is at least of order \(K\) by telescoping. With all path coordinates equal to \(4K\) every link vanishes and the comparator has norm \(4K^{3/2}\). Each link after the anchor belongs to an independently chosen component. [Link](../Proofs/ComponentHessianLink.lean) · [Path and curvature](../Proofs/ComponentHessianChain.lean) · [Objective gap](../Proofs/ComponentHessianGap.lean)

Two obstacles arise with unrestricted queries. A dense query can activate links the algorithm has never visited, and an arbitrarily distant query defeats coordinate hiding based only on a norm bound. The construction cuts off the chain outside a large ball, adds a shared quadratic to preserve convexity throughout the transition, and keeps the full jets of inactive links invisible. Along any displacement, the squared Hessian-variation bounds sum over disjoint owned edges. The path incidence operator has bounded norm, giving a dimension-free bound on the **sum** of squares. This permits a factor \(\sqrt n\) in the objective scale under the **mean**-square assumption. [Localization](../Proofs/ComponentHessianLocalization.lean) · [Component estimates](../Proofs/ComponentHessianFamily.lean) · [Scaled instance](../Proofs/ComponentHessianInstance.lean)

A prefix simulation charges each newly revealed link to finding its owner. For at most \(n(K-1)/8\) queries, at most a quarter of owner strings are exhausted. Counting the hidden coordinates over all queries and the final output loses at most another \(1/32\) of the finite family. On the remaining instances, the actual complete replies agree with the simulated replies and the normalized output gap is at least \(K/n\). Scaling the comparator to radius \(R\) and the Hessian increments to \(H\) gives a gap of order \(HR^3/(\sqrt n K^{7/2})\). Finite averaging over instances before integrating over the algorithm seed gives a fixed defeating instance. For small budgets a separate family hides a quadratic with one of two opposite minimizers in one component. This yields the initialization cost even at \(H=0\), since the model bounds changes in curvature and permits arbitrarily large constant curvature. ## Relation to prior bounds

Tsedenov and Kovalev use the same mean-square condition in (A1). Their Theorem 2 obtains the same powers assuming uniform independent component sampling and the second-order span rule (A2). Section 3.2(ii) explicitly raises removal of the span condition. Here an algorithm can systematically scan indices, revisit selected components, query dense points far from the origin, or read arbitrary information from a complete Hessian. Batching all components gives a full Hessian at cost \(n\) and a full-batch accelerated bound of order \(n[1+(HR^3/\varepsilon)^{2/7}]\). It does not give the finite-sum exponent. The prior restricted-chain argument also does not control arbitrary probes. [Tsedenov and Kovalev, arXiv v1, §§1–3 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.10495v1)

Corollary 1 of that preprint already gives expected objective accuracy with expected work \(W=O(n\log(n+1)+n^{6/7}(HR^3/\varepsilon)^{2/7})\). Its tuning in (13) uses the norm of a minimizer. Given only a positive upper bound \(R\), extend the objective by one unused coordinate. There is then a minimizer of norm exactly \(R\), and every extended-space jet can be simulated with one original-space jet. The algorithm can use \(R\) without knowing that minimizer. At \(H=0\) the average is quadratic and one full scan suffices. [Theorem 1, Corollary 1 and Appendix A.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.10495v1)

The Lean lower bound concerns a fixed cap. For comparison, the value-inclusive oracle gives the following conversion of the published upper bound. The aggregate Hessian is \(H\)-Lipschitz by Cauchy–Schwarz. Scan all \(n\) components at zero and minimize the quadratic Taylor model plus \(H\|x\|^3/6\). Taylor's remainder bound, applied at the model minimizer and at a true minimizer, bounds the returned gap by \(B=HR^3/3\). If \(B\le\varepsilon\), this scan suffices. Otherwise set \(e_j=2^{-j}B\) and \(J=\lceil\log_2(B/\varepsilon)\rceil\). At stage \(j=1,\ldots,J\), independently run the published algorithm from zero at accuracy \(e_j/4\) with cap \(\lceil4W(e_j/4)\rceil\), where \(W\) is a uniform expected-work bound. Keep the new output on completion and the previous output on timeout. Markov's inequality bounds the timeout probability by \(1/4\). Nonnegativity bounds the new gap integrated over completion by \(e_j/4\), even when gap and work are correlated. The old gap is independent of the new timeout. Induction gives expected gap at most \(e_j/4+e_{j-1}/4\le e_j\), starting from the fallback. Selection requires no further queries.

The cap is \(n+\sum_{j=1}^J\lceil4W(e_j/4)\rceil\). Write \(X=HR^3/\varepsilon\). Summing the accuracy-dependent terms geometrically gives \(O(n\log(2n)\log(2+X)+n^{6/7}X^{2/7})\). In particular this is \(O(n\log^2(2n)+n^{6/7}X^{2/7})\). To see this, split the logarithm at \(X=\sqrt n\) and use \(2ab\le a^2+b^2\) and \(\log^2(2+y)=O(1+y^{2/7})\) for \(y=X/\sqrt n\). Thus the fixed-budget comparison matches in order when \(s\ge\log^2(2n)\), and leaves at most logarithmic factors overall. The formal deliverable is the new lower bound. The accuracy-dependent term exceeds the scan term when \(HR^3/\varepsilon>\sqrt n\).

Zhang et al. obtain the different exponent \(5/7\) under individual convexity and a uniform individual Hessian Lipschitz constant. Here a component can have a curvature-change constant as large as \(\sqrt n H\). Woodworth and Srebro's Theorem 7 uses a bounded domain and a different component oracle; the piecewise link in their equation (11) lacks a continuous Hessian. Arjevani and Shamir impose restrictions on using the component Hessians, and their quadratic instances are identifiable from a single full scan of our oracle. Wu et al. treat unrestricted higher-order finite-sum queries for nonconvex gradient stationarity with a different accuracy target. These results do not give this convex expected-gap lower bound.
