# How brightness contrast changes first-fit time for a learned two-tap filter

On the CIFAR-10 airplane/car draw, increasing image brightness relative to spatial detail slows raw logistic gradient descent much more than training a circular two-tap filter and a linear readout together. The mechanism studied here is that a learned filter has two independent responses. Its tap sum transmits brightness. Its tap difference can continue learning a separating spatial direction. A fixed filter with even a small nonzero tap sum continues to transmit brightness as its input amplitude grows.

## The two oracle bounds

Fix all labeled rows and the prescribed initial parameters. Write each signed row as $X_i(R)=R d_i e+z_i$, where $e$ is the constant unit vector and $z_i$ is centered. The circular shift $S$ acts on the flattened image. The network score is the inner product of its readout with $(c_1 I+c_2 S)X_i(R)$. Its dependence on the trainable parameters is bilinear. GD minimizes the average logistic loss, takes both layer gradients at the same point and retains the first iterate with every signed score strictly positive. The raw comparison fixes the identity filter. Each query processes all $n$ rows and returns the descent direction and a strict-fit flag. Data diagnostics and any search for initialization or tuning certificates are preprocessing costs outside this query model. [Joint setup](../Setups/BrightnessLogisticJoint.lean) · [Algorithm](../Algorithms/FirstFitGradientDescent.lean) · [Joint theorem](BrightnessLogisticFirstFit.lean) · [Raw theorem and its separate assumptions](BrightnessLogisticRawLowerBound.md)

Let $A_i=(I-S)z_i/\sqrt2$ and $C_i=(I+S)z_i/\sqrt2$. For the upper bound require a unit-ball separator of the $A_i$ with margin $\gamma>0$, a bound $J\ge1$ on $\|A_i\|+\|C_i\|$, and both signs of brightness with average positive and negative absolute mass at least $\mu>0$. Every nonzero $|d_i|$ lies between $m>0$ and $D$. Write the starting readout as $w+(t/R)e$, with $w$ centered, and the normalized tap sum and difference as $P,Q$. Require $Q>0$ and a vector $a$ with $\langle w-a,A_i\rangle\ge0$ for all rows and $\|a\|^2+4g^2\le Q^2$ for some $g>0$. The mixed row products must satisfy $2B(M+1)^2|\langle C_i,A_j\rangle|\le g^2$. These are static inequalities for images and the specified initialization. The choice of initialization matters since both zero layers, for instance, give a stationary point. No condition is imposed on the observed iterates. The initialization and step pass to the algorithm as public input.

With $L=1+\log(2n)$, $K=3L+1$, $\nu=\gamma g$, define
$$
I=\|a\|+\sqrt{P^2+Q^2+2t^2+3+K},\quad M=1+2I/\nu+4K/\nu^2,
$$
$$
B=[1+3(P^2+t^2)+Q^2+\|w\|^2]\exp[(6L+22J+5J^2)(M+1)],\qquad
\kappa=[4(1+D)(1+JB)]^{-1}.
$$
For all sufficiently large $R$ the constant step $\kappa/R$ reaches strict fit within $1+\lceil 2nRM/\kappa\rceil$ full-batch oracle calls. The five explicit lower-threshold tests for $R$ in the setup control sigmoid saturation, occupation time, layer imbalance, discretization and preservation of tap sign. Their constants are independent of $R$ at fixed cohort. Dimension enters through row, separator and initialization geometry. Neither the exponential constants nor the explicit factor of $n$ give a practical absolute-time forecast. On the intersection with the raw setup, every positive raw step needs $\Omega(R^2/\log R)$ calls. The separation is at least order $R/\log R$ in first-fit complexity. This compares two GD parameterizations of the same classifier on the same rows. A specially designed fixed zero-sum filter can eliminate brightness before GD starts.

The main difficulty is controlling the fast brightness response over many discrete updates while the difference tap learns a separator. Split the actual readout displacement into contributions from the shifted sums and differences. Their inner products are controlled by the static mixed-row condition. When the brightness logit is large, saturation and two-sided brightness mass force a restoring update. A discrete energy estimate bounds how much time the process can spend there. A discounted layer-imbalance estimate charges the remaining work against the cumulative sigmoid weights. These estimates keep the difference tap positive long enough for its separating direction to bound the total vote mass. Each iterate before strict fit spends at least $1/(2n)$ in mean vote, giving the finite first-fit horizon. The companion raw proof instead uses the persistent clearance of an oscillating brightness vote from an integer lattice. [Occupation estimate](../Proofs/BrightnessLogisticResidence.lean) · [Imbalance estimate](../Proofs/BrightnessLogisticImbalance.lean) · [First-fit argument](../Proofs/BrightnessLogisticFirstFit.lean)

Tyurin's Theorems 5.1–5.2 in Section 5 and LongTails PR #253 analyze two-sample perceptron dynamics. A mistake-driven perceptron chooses an example, whereas all signed rows supply nonzero sigmoid votes in the present logistic update. Raw logistic risk bounds also do not determine these first-fit times: loss below $\log(2)/n$ suffices, but a strictly fitting state can have much higher loss. Wu et al. analyze large-step raw logistic loss on separable data. Wen et al. study the transition to stability as the step grows. Neither gives the all-step, fixed-cohort contrast lower bound or the simultaneous learned-filter upper bound here. The implicit preconditioning of overparameterized linear models is an earlier insight. Our contribution is the finite-step first-fit separation as image brightness changes. The earlier rare-mass construction in PR #281 varies row multiplicity and its mechanism gives no corresponding fixed-cohort contrast guarantee. ## A controlled comparison

The experiment varies only each signed image’s constant component, compensates the initial readout to preserve the starting predictions, and preserves both original layer norms across brightness levels. Every trial uses simultaneous finite-step gradients of the full average logistic loss. All architectures receive the same grid of 47 constant step sizes. The first-fit time counts updates to the first iterate with strictly positive margins on every image. Checking initialization and the terminal iterate uses one additional full-batch query in each trial. Searching all 47 rates in parallel through the best hit costs at most 47 times this per-trial cost. The first-fit bound for a prescribed tuning accounts for its own full-batch queries.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 294,338 | 29,747 | 18,849 | 15,255 |
| 2 | 1,021,760 | 104,807 | 23,591 | 30,232 |

For negative initial tap difference (Gisette), simultaneous negation of both trainable layers makes the difference positive and preserves every score and subsequent GD update up to the same sign change. The experimental norm compensation also multiplies all image coordinates by a common gain that approaches a positive constant as brightness grows. The theorem keeps the centered data fixed when varying contrast.

The reflected comparison appends a reversed copy of every flattened image and divides by the square root of two. It preserves all inner products for the raw image rows. A separate lifted readout matches both initial raw and joint scores. To satisfy the theoretical initial balance on CIFAR, multiply both initial joint taps by 1.50 and divide the joint readout by 1.50. This preserves each joint logit and changes the parameterization of the subsequent GD steps. The comparable Gisette factor is 1.28. At brightness factors 1, 2 and 4 this certified Gisette initialization gave 675, 1,918 and 5,382 updates, against 967, 1,382 and 4,295 on the original data. The geometry and the initial balance are verifiable before training. The large-contrast threshold of the asymptotic proof can exceed all three experimental scales.

The shared twelve-step grid and this expanded grid give different numerical speedups. On the shared 5,000-image random draw the twelve-rate reproduction gave 319,563 raw and 23,629 joint updates, a 13.52-fold advantage. Floating-point arithmetic and grid density both affect the measured first-hit index.

## Limits of the mechanism

The native image rows need not have small interactions between their shifted sums and differences. On CIFAR the signed interaction omitted by the reflected model was −2.13% of within-difference growth by the first fit of the step-1/8 trajectory. The corresponding absolute variations had a ratio of 65.62%. On the original Fashion-MNIST 7/9 run at the same step, the signed ratio was −35.38%, with a much larger transient discrepancy. The Fashion initial filter already suppresses brightness and can fit faster than joint training over the tested scales. On MNIST 7/8 with twice the original brightness, raw GD fit in 22,862 updates and joint GD needed 69,401. These cases delimit the explanatory claim.

## Approaches investigated

The experiments ask two different questions. A filter fixed in advance tests what spatial reweighting can accomplish. An explanation of joint GD must also account for the time the two taps and the readout take to learn from their published initialization. The following inventory records the approaches we tested for both questions, including the unsuccessful ones. A *cold* run starts from the prescribed initialization. A *warm* result starts from a specified later state or a separately constructed classifier.

### Measuring the effect and locating its source

| Approach | What the comparison established | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Replicate Figure 1 and repeat step selection | The shared twelve-rate reproduction on the shared airplane/car draw gave 319,563 raw updates and 23,629 joint updates. Common grids, larger grids, shared initial logits, independent draws and higher-precision runs tested sensitivity to tuning and arithmetic. | Grid winners depend on the candidates, arithmetic and draw. Some other class pairs favor raw GD. |
| Freeze a filter or one of the two tap degrees of freedom | Frozen initial taps already improve CIFAR. A preselected high-pass filter beat both separately tuned models on all six focal draws. Radius-only, angle-only, reinforcement and cancellation controls distinguish amplification from spatial reweighting. | These algorithms have different updates. Their hitting times cannot be substituted into the joint bound. |
| Compare margin and covariance before training | Hard margin alone changes little on native CIFAR. The ratio of maximum covariance eigenvalue to squared margin predicted the **raw versus frozen** timing ratio within a factor of 1.46 over 14 dataset and rotation comparisons. | The same statistic misses some learned-model orderings. |
| Change spatial coordinates while controlling the raw problem | Sign blocks, rotations preserving the raw Gram matrix and seeded logits, spectral flattening, and DC or frequency-band controls change the joint advantage. Flattening can reverse the winner. | A particular band or DC effect need not dominate on another dataset. |
| Change factorization, background rows or training stage | Matched initial predictors with different factorizations, matched-predictor restarts, and comparisons from the same later predictor separate the history of training from an individual update. Resampling the background while retaining the hardest images changes the winner. | A favorable one-step comparison or restart does not bound the time from the original initialization. |
| Modify logit temperature | Intervening on already-correct background rows reproduced much of the late advantage of radius learning in a warm comparison. Separate cold retuning largely removed that advantage for an exogenously prescribed temperature. | The late intervention cannot identify a cold iteration rate. |

### Bounding first fit from the labeled rows

| Approach | Established result or obstruction |
|---|---|
| Loss descent and gradient size | An earlier small-step approach used filtered ridge comparators, logistic entropy duality, conic row supports, vote-mass bounds, and coupled curvature, radius and layer-imbalance estimates. Loss bands let their costs vary as training proceeds. It addressed a same-step comparison at small steps and left the independently tuned question open. |
| Raw lower bounds | Static signed combinations of difficult rows, action estimates and comparator interpolation exclude raw first fit for an explicit interval of times. At sufficiently large steps a separate integer-Gram comparison with hard votes gives exact sign histories. A cone-distance calculation sharpens the numerical obstruction at intermediate steps, where one-step worst-case errors still accumulate too quickly. |
| Sharper curvature and radial progress | We tested local Hessian bounds, simultaneous tap/readout expansions, endpoint margins, entropy prices, convex lower supports for radial work, squared layer norms, and a linear readout potential. Uniform bounds on portions of the state space improve small-step estimates. At the operating step, even 32-step loss descent can fail. A state with the same cold taps, risk, DC coordinate and slow-readout norm can have the opposite slow radial increment. |
| Common and moving separators | Positive null combinations of endpoint rows rule out a shared readout separator over a range of filter angles visited on native CIFAR. Narrower sectors admit one. Inverse-filter comparators keep the prediction-space separator fixed, but moving the readout comparator incurs a transport term whose uniform price can exceed the gain. |
| Static first-fit tests | A failed row has a logistic vote of at least one half. Row-specific entropy bonuses, polar-cone distances, multiple ridge prices, and spectral norm constraints strengthen the universal loss threshold. Several such tests detect success well after the first actual fit. Some sufficient regions are never entered on the measured run. |
| Exactly solve for a fitting tap pair given the readout | Intersecting the signed rows’ slope intervals gives an exact feasibility test. On the native high-precision run a fitting tap pair first existed at update 21,733 and joint GD first fit at 23,431. Across the six draws most training elapsed before any tap pair could fit the current readout. The subsequent motion of the interval endpoints also matters. |
| Track the last rows and their background forces | Two-row determinants, separate feature scores, null-space witnesses and warm replays isolate the remaining sign changes. The direct force from the hardest rows can be hundreds of times larger than its residual after the background contribution. Even equal loss, taps, Fourier powers, selected hard-row responses and next taps can yield different first-fit flags one update later. |
| Replace the training state by a smaller set of coordinates | We tried covariance subspaces, ridge and image-moment bases, selected rows, forcing from omitted coordinates, and Bellman bounds on fast coordinates. These give local or conditional controls. Small ridge-only subspaces can miss first fit altogether. A uniform bound must pay for the omitted background votes. |
| Model the fast oscillations | DC and Nyquist channel recurrences, integrated logistic curvature, profiled losses, two-step updates and forced scalar excursions isolate finite-amplitude effects. Distinct phases of one stationary ridge profile can drive the true tap angle in opposite directions. The leading Hessian mode detects many oscillations but also flags quiet periods. |
| Track tap balance and Fourier phase | Swapping the two taps preserves the norm of the filtered gradient. Its associated isometry gives an exact discrete balance with phase, transport and quadratic update terms. Rotating Fourier phases can preserve every frequency’s power and the exact loss while changing strict fit. These identities identify information a progress bound has to retain. |
| Forecast the learned filter and its time from ridge problems | A stability constraint on pretraining ridge solutions predicts the fitted filter shape closely on native CIFAR. Integrating estimated radial work predicts 14,761 updates at step ⅛. Correcting for readout rotation gives 17,880 versus 23,647 in the corresponding measured run. Some forecasts predict success at steps where the actual numerical runs fail. These are empirical forecasts. |
| Distinguish unstable tuning from slow training | Alternating saturated vote masks yield explicit linear maps in two-tap parameter space. On Fashion-MNIST 5/7 at step ⅛ their two-update product has two expanding directions that preserve opposite groups of failed rows. A high-precision cold-prefix calculation and an invariant-region check address this particular failed trial. They supply no upper bound for a successful CIFAR trial. |
| Certify the exact updates directly | High-precision and interval replays can settle a specified run or a neighborhood of a specified state. The earlier trajectory-certificate approach provides a reference for first-fit indices and numerical sensitivity. A mechanism derived from static image measurements must still explain why the cold iterates reach a fitting classifier. |

### Tests of asymptotic explanations on the full draws

A first-step opposition test used the published raw initialization. On CIFAR, 28.54% of signed rows oppose its initial descent direction. Across EuroSAT, Gisette, Fashion 5/7, Fashion 7/9 and MNIST 7/8 the corresponding fractions are 51.18%, 50%, 50%, 34.60% and 48.29%. On CIFAR the opposing rows account for 25.6% of the absolute initial directional work after weighting by their logistic votes. Thus a tiny opposing cohort along the initial gradient is a poor description of this draw. Optimizing the initial deficit over positive combinations of the 64 images ranked hardest before training gives only 3.12 updates of subsequent recovery time if each future vote is constrained solely to lie between zero and one. Any longer lower bound using those rows must make further use of the update dynamics.

We also tested whether the filter could learn to classify difficult images from the other rows alone. We excluded the first 1, 4, 16 or 64 images in the same pretraining ranking and fitted maximum-margin classifiers on the remaining rows for seven fixed filter correlations from −0.99 to 0.99. The fitted classifiers had a negative margin on at least one excluded row in every one of the 168 combinations of dataset, cohort size and filter. On CIFAR, when only the hardest image was excluded, its best measured signed margin across the seven filters was −7.31, in units where the smallest retained-row margin was one. This calculation concerns static max-margin solutions. It leaves open transient fits and focuses attention on how the difficult rows' own gradients interact with the background.

We examined brightness as a pretraining property by multiplying each image's constant component by a common factor and compensating the initial readout. Both models retain their initial signed scores and both layer norms. On the CIFAR draw, doubling brightness raised the frozen-initial-filter first-fit count from 29,747 to 104,807, and the joint count from 18,849 to 23,591, on the same grid of 47 constant learning rates. At fourfold brightness the frozen initial filter reached first fit in 149 and 280 updates on the Fashion-MNIST 5/7 and 7/9 draws, respectively. Joint training took 14,715 and 12,745. These measurements support a brightness-sensitive advantage of tap learning on CIFAR. They also show why a raw-versus-joint speed comparison alone cannot attribute that advantage to tap learning across datasets.

A mathematical idealization splits centered image differences and neighbor sums into orthogonal feature spaces. On unmodified CIFAR, a normalized difference row and a normalized sum row have absolute inner product above 0.50. At least one of their relative projection errors must exceed 0.25 for every proposed orthogonal split, by the triangle and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities. Appending a reversed copy of each flattened image gives an exact split by reflection symmetry while retaining every example and its label. We found unit vectors with positive margins on the difference rows of all six mirror-extended draws. On CIFAR the minimum was 0.00205. The mirror extension gives a controlled comparison with an idealized dataset. It changes the feature geometry of the original experiment.

An additional reflection control preserves both the raw and the two-tap initial scores by solving for the readout on the extended features. On the ordinary brightness scale, the joint first-fit counts were 18,849 on CIFAR and 18,164 on its matched-score reflection. On Gisette the counts were 967 and 966. Doubling and quadrupling Gisette brightness gave 1,382 and 4,295 updates on the original data, and 1,381 and 4,289 on the matched-score reflection. This agreement is an empirical property of the control. Matching scores changes the readout, and the initialization used in this control can violate the sufficient imbalance condition. On the Fashion-MNIST 7/9 draw, the corresponding fourfold counts differed more substantially at 12,745 and 8,833.

There is also a direct check along the native joint trajectory. Decompose each readout increment into the neighbor-difference and neighbor-sum contributions delivered by the actual batch gradient. In the reflected model these vectors belong to orthogonal spaces. On the 23,431-update CIFAR run at step 1/8, the cumulative contribution from the sum component to squared difference-tap growth was −5.98, against +280.57 from the difference component. Its magnitude was 2.13% of that within-component contribution at first fit. Summing absolute contributions instead gives a ratio of 65.62%. The CIFAR agreement depends on cancellation over training, and the first-fit ratio understates transient discrepancies. On Gisette the signed and absolute ratios were −1.55% and 1.56%. These trajectory measurements test an implication of the idealization. They do not serve as data assumptions for the convergence proof.

The Figure 1 reproduction uses the paper’s separate grids in FP32, six steps for raw and seven for joint. On the documented shared draw it gives 319,077 and 23,676 updates (13.48×). On the twelve-rate common grid in FP64, the respective native counts on all six draws are:

These are training first fits on class-pair cohorts. Increasing brightness by four on CIFAR increases raw training time by 13.54× versus 3.28× for joint on the expanded grid. The reflected model with certified initial balance differs from native joint by −19.1%, +28.2% and +0.013% over the three contrast levels. This tests the geometric approximation at finite contrasts. It leaves open whether native images obey a weaker mixed-row condition that would suffice for a comparable theorem. The CIFAR seed is 20261125 and samples 2,500 images per class without replacement. From the experiment directory, use Python with NumPy, CUDA-enabled PyTorch, Pillow, Matplotlib and python-flint. The replay reads public training files, reconstructs the exact signed rows by their ordered SHA-256 fingerprints and uses the archived initial readout and taps. CIFAR uses the Toronto binary archive, EuroSAT the RGB JPEG zip, Gisette the UCI zip, and Fashion-MNIST and MNIST their training IDX gzip pairs. Substitute the file or directory and dataset alias as appropriate. The timings used an H100; floating-point reduction order can affect the first strict fit. Fit at update zero counts as one oracle call.
