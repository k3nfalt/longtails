# Statistical time under weak interpolation

How much stochastic information can heterogeneous workers supply per second when they share a minimizer, but different workers may determine different coordinates? A speed-weighted gradient can point toward a minimizer of the wrong objective on general heterogeneous data. Weak interpolation makes reweighting possible. The issue is how much curvature survives a change of weights when only the *average* objective has quadratic growth.

## Problem and guarantee

Worker $i$ holds a differentiable convex function $f_i$ with gradient Lipschitz constant at most $L_i$. The common solution set $S=\bigcap_i\arg\min f_i$ contains a point within distance $R$ of the public initial point. The average objective satisfies $f(x)-\min f\ge\mu\,\mathrm{dist}(x,S)^2/2$. Each call at worker $i$ takes $h_i>0$ seconds and yields an unbiased gradient of $f_i$ with centered second moment at most $\sigma^2$. The jointly measurable oracles have IID sample streams, independent between workers. The setup uses an abstract joint sampling law. A minimizer, local function values, and the individual curvatures realized by an instance are private. The bounds $L_i,\mu,R,\sigma$, service times, and target $\varepsilon>0$ are public. [Lean setup](../Setups/DistributedWeakInterpolation.lean)

Define the public profile by choosing $c\ge0$ with $\sum_i\min(L_i,c h_i)=n\mu$, and put
$$
u_i=\min(L_i,c h_i),\quad w_i=u_i/h_i,\quad I=\sum_i u_i^2/h_i,\quad
A=\max_i u_i L_i,\quad C=2 A\sum_i h_i^{-1},\quad
\kappa=C/I,\quad \Gamma=\sigma\max_i u_i/A.
$$
Monotonicity and continuity give a threshold for all valid public parameters, including equality at saturation. The resulting profile is unique even when the threshold is nonunique. It can be obtained by sorting the breakpoints $L_i/h_i$. Different workers saturate as $\mu$ passes those breakpoints, so a single scalar threshold expresses the active set for arbitrary worker profiles. The finite-dimensional space and public $L_i$ may include zero-dimensional directions and workers with $L_i=0$. [Design](../Algorithms/WeakInterpolationDesign.lean) · [Profile proof](../Proofs/InterpolationWeights.lean)

The theorem returns the **iterate after the $2J$ scheduled SGD updates**. The index is fixed in advance and the iterate depends on the samples. It uses the actual gradients, bit strings, and event times of a coordinator–worker star. For every admissible instance it gives $\mathbb E\,\mathrm{dist}(x_{\rm out},S)^2\le\varepsilon$. With $H=\max_i h_i$, per-message latency $\ell$, and per-bit time $b$, the explicit Lean deadline is, up to universal factors,
$$
\frac{\sigma^2}{I\varepsilon}
+ \kappa\bigl[1+\log(1+R^2/\varepsilon)\bigr]
\left[H+\ell+b\left(64+d\log\left(2+d+\kappa+
\frac{R^2+\Gamma^2}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right].
$$
The integer bit length in the Lean formula implements the logarithm. Independent directed links support concurrent transmissions to distinct workers. Both transmissions and both 64-bit clock headers are charged in every round. The statistical term has no multiplier logarithmic in accuracy. When $\sigma=0$, only the initialization and communication term remains. ## Why these weights work

At a query $x\notin S$, choose a closest $y\in S$. Normalize each local gap by half the squared distance. The resulting numbers $a_i$ obey $0\le a_i\le L_i$ by interpolation and smoothness, and $\sum_i a_i\ge n\mu$ by global quadratic growth. The least favorable profile for the information problem is
$$
I=\min\left\{\sum_i a_i^2/h_i:0\le a_i\le L_i,\ \sum_i a_i\ge n\mu\right\}.
$$
Indeed $w_i=c$ off the capped indices. On a capped index $w_i\le c$ and $a_i-u_i\le0$. Consequently $\sum_i w_i(a_i-u_i)\ge c\sum_i(a_i-u_i)\ge0$. Expanding the square proves optimality. The **same supporting inequality** gives quadratic growth $I$ for the surrogate $\sum_iw_i f_i$, even though the local gap profile can change with the direction and query point. A unit-time variance calculation for its gradient pays $\sum_i w_i^2 h_i=I$. This simultaneous curvature and variance certificate explains the leading time. It uses only shared optimality and growth of the aggregate. [Nonlinear geometry](../Proofs/WeakInterpolationGeometry.lean)

The protocol batches in proportion to worker speed and rescales each local reply before transmission. For a phase length $J\asymp\kappa[1+\log(1+R^2/\varepsilon)]$, it gives each batch time proportional to $\max(H,\sigma^2/(I\varepsilon J))$, rounded to an integer count for each worker. It takes $J$ constant steps to reduce the initial error and then $J$ diminishing steps. The latter phase reduces the stochastic contribution without paying the initialization logarithm on every required sample. All $2J+1$ batches executed by the underlying protocol are charged, including the final batch after the returned point has been computed. The final internal state reaches index $2J+1$ and the outbox carries index $2J$. Finite-variance clipping of each batch and two finite-grid roundings introduce bias. Their error grows with distance from the initial point. A second recurrence controls the expected squared distance to one fixed common minimizer, which makes the accumulated rounding error small enough even for unbounded sample noise and an unbounded solution set. [Batch moments](../Proofs/WeightedInterpolationBatch.lean) · [Adaptive descent](../Proofs/WeightedInterpolationAdaptive.lean) · [Actual execution](../Proofs/WeightedInterpolationExecution.lean)

One application is distributed estimation of a common parameter from complementary sensors. Worker $i$ observes $A_i\theta+\xi_i$ with centered, independent measurement noise and computes $A_i^\top(A_i x-A_i\theta-\xi_i)$. The noise variance depends on $A_i$ and the sensor covariance, uniformly in $x$. Individual sensors may miss directions, while the stacked measurement matrix gives quadratic growth on its row space.

For a nonlinear example, take unit vectors $a,b$ with $0<|a^\top b|<1$, and two local objectives $f_1(x)=(a^\top x)^2/2+\phi(b^\top x)$ and $f_2(x)=(b^\top x)^2/2$, where $\phi(0)=\phi'(0)=0$ and $\phi''(t)=t^2/(1+t^2)$. The average dominates the quadratic frame operator on the span of $a,b$, so it has positive quadratic growth relative to the orthogonal complement. The local minimum sets differ. Along $x=t(b-(a^\top b)a)$, the first function vanishes quartically near zero and its gradient vanishes cubically, excluding any positive local PL constant. Sums of rotated blocks give interacting nonlinear instances of arbitrarily large dimension. The proof needs neither a common Hessian eigenbasis nor a uniform directional allocation of curvature.

## Prior results, comparison regimes, and limitations

Tyurin's September 2026 preprint studies this question with weak interpolation in Assumption 3.1, convex smooth local functions, fixed service times, and global PL. For smooth convex objectives PL and quadratic growth give one another up to universal factors. Theorem 3.2 gives a slow-worker obstruction in the regime $\mu<L_{\max}/(2n)$. Theorem 3.8 obtains a harmonic-time guarantee under *both* equal local minimizer sets and individual PL (Assumptions 3.3 and 3.4). Its biased-gradient argument uses those assumptions to give each worker a common lower curvature. The information profile here instead describes the worst distribution permitted by a sum constraint and individual upper bounds. In the uncapped regime $u_i=n\mu h_i/\sum_j h_j$, the statistical time reduces to $\sigma^2\sum_i h_i/[(n\mu)^2\varepsilon]$, consistent with the slow-worker regime. The gain occurs when smoothness caps force signal away from slow workers. Communication in this comparison adds an explicit term in our star. [Tyurin, Sections 1–3 and proofs of Theorems 3.2, 3.7, and 3.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17483v1)

Classical parallel batching with $t/h_i$ calls per worker estimates each local mean at variance at most $\sigma^2h_i/t$. Keeping the uniform global gradient costs the sum of these variances divided by $n^2$; normalizing by global growth alone recovers the uncapped expression above. Rescaled ASGD corrects unequal arrival rates to recover the uniform objective under heterogeneous data. Inkheart's worker weighting optimizes the cost of estimating a common gradient in its homogeneous model. Their statistical analyses do not supply the curvature certificate for a biased mixture of different objectives. The arbitrary-computation model also covers time-dependent availability beyond the fixed-time assumption here. [Rescaled ASGD, Sections 2–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13434v1) · [Inkheart/M4, Section 4 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07795v2) · [Tyurin, ICLR 2025](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=cUN8lJB4rD)

For a quantitative comparison let $n=2N,L_i=1,\mu=1/2$. Give $N$ workers unit time and the remaining $N$ times $j=1,\ldots,N$. The threshold solves $Nc=\sum_{j=1}^N(1-cj)_+$, hence $c=\Theta(N^{-1/2})$. The capped tail contributes $\sum_{j\gtrsim\sqrt N}1/j=\Theta(\log N)$ to $I$; the other terms contribute $O(1)$. A uniform fastest prefix of $N+j$ workers guarantees total curvature $j$ and has noise cost $N+j(j+1)/2$, giving information at most two. Shorter prefixes can have zero guaranteed curvature. With $d\ge 3$ fixed, $R=\sigma=\ell=b=1$ and $\varepsilon=N^{-3}$, our elapsed time is $\Theta(N^3/\log N)$, versus order $N^3$ for the best uniform-prefix certificate. Startup costs are $O(N^2+N\log N)$ here. The comparison concerns the certificate for a uniform aggregate. An algorithm retaining each message separately can implement other weights.

A two-point calculation identifies the statistical limit even with free, instantaneous communication. For $d\ge1$ use local quadratics of curvature $u_i$ in one coordinate and common center $\theta=\pm2\sqrt\varepsilon$. Each raw oracle reply in that coordinate is $u_i x+\sigma Z$, where an independent sign $Z$ has mean $-u_i\theta/\sigma$. All other coordinates are flat. This is an unbiased oracle with variance at most $\sigma^2$, and it uses the same two-point sample space in both experiments. Start at zero, assume $R\ge2\sqrt\varepsilon$, $\sigma>0$, and $2\max_i u_i\sqrt\varepsilon\le\sigma/2$. At deadline $T$ a worker has at most $\lfloor T/h_i\rfloor$ observations. Even the entire collection of these potential observations has KL divergence at most $32T\varepsilon I/\sigma^2$ between the two centers: for a sign of mean $a\in[-1/2,1/2]$, the divergence from the sign of mean $-a$ is $a\log[(1+a)/(1-a)]\le 4a^2$. The output is a randomized function of these observations with the known offsets restored at the queried points. For $T<\sigma^2/(256I\varepsilon)$, Pinsker's inequality gives equal-prior testing error greater than $1/4$. Squared-distance error at most $\varepsilon$ at each center would give error at most $1/4$ by testing the sign of the output. This benchmark is a derivation in this note. The formal public result is the upper bound. The noisy quadratic experiment is closely related to Tyurin's proof of Theorem 3.7; optimizing its profile under the smoothness caps is essential here. [Tyurin, proof of Theorem 3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17483v1)

Optimality concerns the leading statistical term for small positive accuracy under fixed nondegenerate parameters. Startup, the dimension-dependent finite-bit term, and the deterministic regime have no matching lower bound here. The implementation uses $C$, which may greatly exceed the true smoothness of the surrogate. For $L=(0,1),\mu=1/2,h=(s,1)$, it gives $C=2(1+1/s)$ even though only the second worker has positive weight. A larger initial radius, increased dimension, or a costly link can also dominate the statistical improvement.

## Impression of the distributed protocol

The event model makes a useful distinction between computing an iterate and delivering it. Finite packets, bidirectional transport, and a proof of finite events at every earlier deadline exposed an entire extra batch in this implementation. Independent links and free local arithmetic make the information calculation interpretable. They also mean shared bandwidth, coordinator serialization, expensive local transforms, and variable service times require separate models before making a hardware prediction.
